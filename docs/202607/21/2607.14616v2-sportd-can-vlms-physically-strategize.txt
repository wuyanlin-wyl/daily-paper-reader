## ABSTRACT 

Vision–language models have become increasingly capable of interpreting visual scenes, but it remains unclear whether they can use information to make strate-gically effective decisions. We investigate this question in soccer, where models observe the seconds preceding an on-ball decision and must choose whether to shoot or pass to a specific teammate. Unlike conventional visual-understanding tasks, soccer enables decisions to be evaluated quantitatively by estimating the value of every available action. We introduce SportD , a benchmark comprising 478 on-ball decisions from the 2022 FIFA World Cup. Each model choice is evaluated against a possession-value model that estimates the action that most in-creases the attacking team’s probability of scoring, allowing us to measure both optimal-action accuracy and the value forfeited by suboptimal decisions. Across three frontier VLMs, the best selects the highest-valued action on 31 .4% of events, compared with 38 .9% for the professional players, and all models incur signifi-cantly greater regret. Further analysis reveals a systematic preference for lower-variance and lower-reward actions: VLMs shoot less often and select substantially less progressive passes than either the optimal policy or the real players. The models also reproduce the player’s specific action above chance even when that action is suboptimal, suggesting partial imitation of familiar play patterns rather than consistent evaluation of counterfactual alternatives. SportD provides a value-grounded testbed for measuring physical strategic reasoning in VLMs. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Vision-language models (VLMs) have made substantial progress in describing visual scenes, yet physical strategic reasoning remains considerably less developed. This capability requires models to integrate fine-grained spatiotemporal perception with forward-looking evaluation: they must not only identify what is happening, but also determine which action is most advantageous given the evolving state of the environment. Such reasoning is essential for agents to operate in the physical world, but it is largely absent from benchmarks centered on recognition, captioning, and visual question answering, which evaluate representations of a scene rather than decisions within it. Team sports provide a particularly well-structured setting in which to study this problem. Each on-ball moment constitutes a compact multi-agent decision task involving more than ten players in continuous motion, a constrained set of feasible actions, and an expert decision-maker who selected one action under the same observable conditions presented to the model. Sports also offer an un-usually objective basis for evaluating strategic quality. Possession-value models such as Valuing Actions by Estimating Probabilities (VAEP) (Decroos et al., 2019; Fernández et al., 2021) estimate the change in a team’s probability of scoring associated with each candidate action. Recent benchmarks have begun to probe VLMs in sport and in strategy, but from different angles. Sports-understanding suites such as SPORTU (Xia et al., 2025) and SportR (Xia et al., 2026) evalu-ate perception, rule comprehension, and multi-step reasoning about sports content, largely through description and question answering rather than forward-looking action choice scored against a value function. Strategic-ability benchmarks such as VS-Bench (Xu et al., 2026) test VLMs as agents in multi-agent games, but in abstract or simulated environments rather than grounded physical deci-sions read from real broadcast perception. What is missing is a setting that asks the VLM to make the actual on-ball decision from raw perception and grades it by the value it forgoes relative to the optimal action, physical strategic reasoning with a real, objective payoff. We introduce SportD , 478 on-ball decisions from the 2022 FIFA World Cup (Fig. 1). For each, a VLM sees the seconds of play leading up to the moment and must choose one action, pass to a specific teammate, or shoot. Because every candidate action carries a value, we score not just whether the VLM found the optimum but how much value it left on the table ( regret ), and we compare that, per event, to what the real player left. Our findings are consistent and informative about the state of physical strategic reasoning. (i) All three frontier VLMs we test make significantly worse decisions than the real World Cup players, leaving more value on the table and sitting between a random baseline and the human on accuracy and skill. (ii) Reading the value model’s own success probabilities, the VLMs are systematically 

risk-averse , they pick lower-variance and lower-reward actions than the optimum on 63 –72% of events, reaching for less upside than both the optimum and the human players. (iii) Imitation of the human choice is real across the board: all three VLMs reproduce the player’s exact choice well above chance from perception alone, most strongly GPT. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Vision–language models, perception, and reasoning. Modern VLMs pair a visual encoder with a language model (Radford et al., 2021; Alayrac et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023b), and the strongest systems (OpenAI, 2023; Gemini Team, Google, 2023; Bai et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024b) inherit the in-context reasoning of their language backbones (Brown et al., 2020; Wei et al., 2022; Kojima et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2023). A large body of benchmarks probes what they perceive: compositional and relational reasoning (Johnson et al., 2017; Antol et al., 2015; Hudson & Manning, 2019), spatial relations (Liu et al., 2023a; Chen et al., 2024a), expert multi-discipline knowledge (Yue et al., 2024), perceptual failure modes that persist even in frontier VLMs (Fu et al., 2024; Tong et al., 2024), and the temporal axis in video and egocentric suites (Xiao et al., 22021; Mangalam et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024; Grauman et al., 2022). These test perception and question answering; SportD asks whether that reasoning carries from describing a scene to acting 

well within it, requiring a value-scored action on top of perception. 

Physical and strategic reasoning. Predicting how a physical scene will unfold is a long-standing probe of machine intuition (Battaglia et al., 2013; Bakhtin et al., 2019; Yi et al., 2020; Bear et al., 2021; Piloto et al., 2022), and the world-models line learns latent dynamics to plan or imagine (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2025; Bruce et al., 2024; LeCun, 2022). Separately, game-playing systems have reached or exceeded human level in Go, chess, poker, Stratego, and real-time strategy (Silver et al., 2016; 2018; Brown & Sandholm, 2019; Perolat et al., 2022; Vinyals et al., 2019; Berner et al., 2019), and language-conditioned agents now negotiate and cooperate (Bakhtin et al., 2022; Park et al., 2023); most operate on symbolic or simulator state, and the recent VS-Bench (Xu et al., 2026) evaluates VLMs as strategic agents but in abstract multi-agent environments. A soccer decision joins the two: the right action depends on where players will move and on which move most raises the chance of scoring, read from real broadcast perception rather than a simulator. 

Sports analytics, imitation, and evaluation. Possession-value and space-control VLMs quantify decisions in soccer and basketball (Decroos et al., 2019; Fernández et al., 2021; Cervone et al., 2016; Fernández & Bornn, 2018; Spearman, 2018; Lucey et al., 2015; Decroos & Davis, 2019), providing the objective yardstick our benchmark relies on, while multimodal sports benchmarks target perception and comprehension, action spotting and holistic broadcast understanding (Giancola et al., 2018; Deliège et al., 2021) and VLM sports-understanding suites (Xia et al., 2025; 2026); to our knowledge none scores forward-looking on-ball decisions against a possession-value model. Finally, behavioral cloning reproduces expert actions without regard to their value (Pomerleau, 1991; Ross et al., 2011; Ho & Ermon, 2016), vision–language–action and generalist agents extend this to embodied control (Reed et al., 2022; Driess et al., 2023; Brohan et al., 2023), and whether a model genuinely evaluates or merely imitates its inputs also underlies concerns about LLM judges (Zheng et al., 2023); our ∆-stratified design and imitation rate (Sec. 3.3) are built to tell the two apart. 

## 3 SPORT D

3.1 DATASET 

SportD measures physical strategic reasoning , using the test case of whether a VLM can look at a live sports scene and choose the action a real expert would, not merely say what the scene contains. Each item in SportD is thus a single on-ball decision. The VLM watches the moment and its run-up, commits to one action, and is graded against that value model. The rest of this section describes the data and the views the VLM is shown, the value model that supplies those prices, and the metrics that turn a choice into a score. SportD contains 478 on-ball decisions from the 2022 FIFA World Cup. Each decision e is a single moment at which the player on the ball must act, and we present it to the VLM through three aligned views of the play leading up to that moment: (i) images from the previous ∼ 5 seconds, sampled at 0.5 second intervals, which carry the motion and build-up; (ii) an image of the decision moment itself; and (iii) a top-down map showing the positions of all visible players. Together these give the VLM the same spatial and temporal picture the player had, while the map’s lettered, name-free markers withhold player and team identity, so the VLM must reason from the configuration on the pitch rather than from who is on it. The VLM selects exactly one action ˆa from the available action set Ae, where 

Ae = {SHOOT } ∪ { pass to teammate ℓ : ℓ ∈ Te}. (1) Here, Te is the set of visible teammates in the image. The median event offers approximately six candidate actions. 3.2 VALUE MODEL 

We value actions with VAEP (Decroos et al., 2019). Each match is first converted into SPADL, a unified action representation on a 105 × 68 m pitch in which every on-ball event (pass, dribble, shot, 3and so on) becomes a row with an action type, start and end location, body part, and outcome. VAEP scores an action by the change it produces in the acting team’s chance of scoring. From the current game state, one model estimates the probability that the team scores within the next ten actions and another the probability that it concedes, and the state value is their difference, V = Pscore − Pconcede .The value of an action is the increase in V from just before to just after it. These scoring and conceding models, together with the companion expected-goals and pass-completion models that supply the success probability of each untaken action, are gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost; 100 trees, depth 3). The full stack is trained once on a disjoint set of tournaments, Euro 2020 and Euro 2024, and then applied unchanged to the 2022 World Cup. We divide the events into three approximately equal groups based on how much worse the player’s actual action was than the highest-valued action (Sec. 3.3). The low group contains 163 events where the player chose the highest-valued action, the medium group contains 163 events with a moderate gap, and the high group contains 152 events with the largest gap. 3.3 METRICS

## 4 RESULTS 

4.1 FRONTIER VLM S ARE LESS OPTIMAL THAN PLAYERS 

We evaluate three frontier VLMs, Gemini 3.5 Flash, GPT 5.6 Sol, and Claude Opus 4.8 on SportD, scored by the pipeline of Sec. 3.3. As shown in Table 1, no VLM matches the skill and accuracy of the actual players. The best VLM reaches 31 .4% accuracy and skill 0.24 , versus 38 .9% and 0.32 for the real players, while RANDOM sits at 19 .3% and skill 0.01 .The gap does not stem from the harshness of exact-match scoring. Even if crediting a choice when-ever it ranks among the best X% of the available actions (top-X% accuracy), the core finding is preserved. At top-20% the VLMs reach 35 .6–42 .9% versus 52 .5% for the real player, and the or-dering holds at every threshold of the full rank-percentile curve (Fig. 2). Relaxing the metric raises all systems together but does not close the gap between the VLMs and the player. 4.2 VLM S CONSISTENTLY CARRY OUT RISK -AVERSE ACTIONS 

Using each action’s outcome variance under the value model (Eq. 6), Table 2 shows that every VLM chooses lower-variance actions than the real players. As a within-event paired test, each VLM also chooses a lower-variance action than the optimum on 63 –72% of events (one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank, p < 10 −40 throughout). Beyond variance, the VLMs fall below the players on upside and spread and shoot less often. ## 5 CONCLUSION 

We introduce SportD, a benchmark for evaluating whether VLMs can make strategically valuable decisions from real-world visual observations. Rather than testing whether a model can describe a play or recognize what has already happened, SportD asks it to choose what an on-ball player should do next and evaluates that decision against the estimated value of every available action. Across 478 decisions from the 2022 FIFA World Cup, all three frontier VLMs fall short of the professional players who faced the same situations. Our analysis also reveals systematic structure in these errors. The VLMs consistently favor lower-variance and lower-reward actions, shooting less often and selecting passes that are less progressive than either the optimal action or the action taken by the real player. This suggests that current VLMs do not merely fail unpredictably; they exhibit a recognizable form of strategic conservatism that causes them to avoid actions with greater potential upside. At the same time, the models reproduce the human player’s specific choice more frequently than they identify the value-model optimum, even though player and team identities are withheld. This pattern is especially pronounced for GPT and remains visible in situations where the player’s action is substantially suboptimal. Models may recognize familiar patterns of play and imitate the action those patterns typically produce without fully evaluating the counterfactual value of the available alternatives. SportD nevertheless represents an initial study with several important limitations. First, our coverage is restricted to the men’s 2022 World Cup because complete match footage and sufficiently detailed event and tracking data are not consistently available across competitions. Our action-value pipeline relies on relatively simple gradient-boosted models and necessarily provides an approximation rather than an unquestionable ground-truth measure of strategic value. Future versions could incorporate richer spatiotemporal value models, larger training corpora, uncertainty estimates, and robustness analyses across alternative valuation methods. Finally, the current action space is limited to passing and shooting. Actions such as carrying, dribbling, holding the ball, and moving into space are difficult to represent as a finite set because their targets, paths, and durations lie in a continuous space. Extending SportD to these actions will require models and evaluation procedures that can generate and value continuous trajectories. Addressing these limitations would turn SportD from a focused test of discrete on-ball decisions into a broader evaluation of strategic physical agency.

Below is the exact prompt sent to each VLM. The run-up is presented as a sequence of still frames sampled from the seconds before the decision, one every 0.5 s (Sec. 3). Braced fields ( {...} ) are filled per event: the number of run-up frames and their spacing, the attacking direction, and the letters of the teammates that can legally be passed to. 

You are an elite football (soccer) analyst. Watch this moment and decide what the player on the ball should do next - the choice that best helps his team score soon without giving the ball away cheaply. Judge it on what is visible at the moment the ball is played, not on hindsight. You are shown one moment from a match, three ways: 1. RUN-UP - {n} still frames from the seconds leading up to the moment, one every {interval} seconds of real time, in chronological order. Read them as a sequence: they show who is moving where, and how fast. 2. FINAL FRAME - the broadcast frame at the exact instant the decision is made. This is the decision point. 3. PLAYER MAP - a top-down view of that same instant. Every player on camera is a lettered circle. Blue = the on-ball team (T). Red = the opponents (O). The gold star is the player on the ball. The arrow shows which way the on-ball team is attacking. THE DECISION The player on the ball is the gold star. {attack} What should he do RIGHT NOW? Your options are: - PASS to one of his lettered teammates (the blue circles on the map). The teammates you can pass to are: {teammates}. - SHOOT. Reason it through first: read the shape of the defence, who is actually free, who is covered or offside, where the space is. Ask whether a shot from here beats the best available pass. Say concretely why your choice beats the obvious alternatives. Then end your reply with this line and nothing after it: ANSWER: <letter> where <letter> is one teammate letter (e.g. ANSWER: E) or the word SHOOT.
