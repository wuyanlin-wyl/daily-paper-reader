Abstract 

Vision-language pretraining objectives differ in the spatial granularity of their supervision, yet the implications of this distribution for chest X-ray interpretation remain underex-plored. We present a controlled study that isolates the pre-training objective: holding the encoder and pretraining data fixed, we train nine objectives spanning global and local con-trastive learning, captioning, and their combinations, and eval-uate across five chest X-ray tasks of increasing spatial granu-larity. We find that (i) pretraining granularity aligns with task granularity at the extremes, with local objectives leading on abnormality detection and global objectives on classification; (ii) local objectives are surprisingly competitive on global-level generation and question-answering tasks; (iii) the merits of captioning and contrastive learning reverse across granu-larity levels; and (iv) among combinations, mixing captioning and contrastive supervision is strongest on classification and in-distribution generation, while pairing two captioning objec-tives generalizes best on zero-shot report generation. These results show that no single objective is universally optimal, and that the interaction of objective type, granularity, and task governs downstream performance. 

# Introduction 

Chest X-rays are the most widely used medical imaging pro-cedure worldwide, valued for their accessibility and ease of use (Rajpurkar et al. 2017). However, growing demand has increased the workload on the limited number of radiologists who interpret these images. Radiologists perform a diverse range of tasks depending on the clinical context, from iden-tifying abnormalities to localizing findings and generating detailed diagnostic reports (Akhter, Singh, and Vatsa 2023). Machine learning models have shown considerable promise in chest X-ray interpretation, offering an opportunity to alle-viate this burden. This is especially valuable in low-resource settings, where radiologist shortages are most acute. Despite impressive benchmark results, clinical deploy-ment of these models remains limited (Dutta et al. 2025). Medical vision-language models typically employ frozen vi-sion encoders pretrained with contrastive learning (Radford et al. 2021), applying the same representations across all chest X-ray interpretation tasks. A fundamental challenge is that these encoders often fail to extract sufficiently rich and task-relevant representations (Tong et al. 2024). We argue

Chest X-ray interpretation tasks exhibit substantial varia-tion in their granularity requirements, which can be arranged along a spectrum of spatial granularity as shown in Figure 1. At one extreme, global-level tasks such as disease clas-sification rely on high-level semantic understanding of the entire image. At the other end, local-level tasks such as ab-normality detection and phrase grounding require spatially-grounded representations that focus on specific anatomical regions. Other tasks, such as report generation, visual ques-tion answering (VQA), and Medical Difference VQA (Hu et al. 2023b) fall between the two extremes, requiring both global and local representations in varying degrees. Vision encoders are pretrained using vision-language ob-jectives that vary along the same axis. Global objectives, such as image-text contrastive learning (Radford et al. 2021) and image captioning (Tschannen et al. 2023), incentivize learning coarse semantic representations aligned with global image content. While effective for high-level understanding tasks, these objectives provide limited supervision for learn-ing spatially precise visual representations. In contrast, lo-cal objectives, such as referring expression comprehension, phrase grounding, and local contrastive learning, incorporate region-level supervision that explicitly aligns regional cap-tions with local image regions, resulting in more spatially grounded representations (Wan et al. 2024). Despite this parallel, the extent to which pretraining granularity affects downstream performance across the task spectrum remains underexplored In this work, we systematically investigate a fundamen-tal question: Do local vision-language pretraining objec-tives learn visual representations that are better suited for spatially grounded chest X-ray interpretation than global objectives, and does this advantage increase as tasks demand finer spatial granularity? To answer this question, we perform a controlled empirical study in which we hold the vision encoder architecture fixed and vary only the pretraining objective. We evaluate the resulting repre-sentations across five downstream chest X-ray interpretation tasks: classification, report generation, VQA, medical dif-ference VQA, and abnormality detection. # Related Work 

Vision-Language Pretraining 

Traditionally, vision encoders were trained using supervised learning, which requires large amounts of manually anno-tated data and is therefore costly to scale. Inspired by the success of self-supervised learning in natural language pro-cessing, computer vision increasingly shifted toward leverag-ing large collections of image-text pairs available on the web. This shift motivated the development of vision-language pre-training methods that learn aligned representations of images and text. Early approaches, such as CLIP (Radford et al. 2021), demonstrated that contrastive learning can produce vision encoders whose performance rivals that of supervised models. Subsequent methods, including SigLIP (Zhai et al. 2023), further validated the effectiveness of image-text align-ment objectives. Generative objectives based on image cap-tioning (Tschannen et al. 2023) have since emerged as a strong alternative to contrastive pretraining, particularly for multimodal downstream tasks. More recent work (Xiao et al. 2024; Wan et al. 2024) has explored combining multiple pre-training objectives to capture information at different levels of semantic granularity within a shared vision encoder. 

Global and Localized Vision-Language Alignment 

Global alignment objectives (Radford et al. 2021; Zhai et al. 2023; Tschannen et al. 2023) learn correspondences between entire images and their associated reports or captions. Meth-ods such as contrastive learning (Radford et al. 2021) and im-age captioning (Tschannen et al. 2023) operate at the image level, encouraging the model to learn semantic representa-tions that capture the overall content of an image. While these approaches have demonstrated strong performance across a variety of downstream tasks, they do not explicitly model the relationship between specific image regions and textual descriptions. Local alignment objectives (Wan et al. 2024) instead in-duce correspondences between image regions and local-ized textual references, through tasks such as automatic referring expression comprehension, grounded captioning, and region-level contrastive learning. By providing explicit spatial supervision, these objectives encourage spatially grounded representations that may be better suited to lo-calization and fine-grained reasoning. Recent work (Huang et al. 2021) has further shown that combining global and local objectives improves performance on multimodal tasks, suggesting that the spatial granularity of the pretraining sig-nal plays an important role in the quality of the learned representations. 

# Method 

Our goal is to investigate how the spatial granularity of vision-language pretraining objectives affects downstream chest X-ray interpretation. To isolate the effect of the objec-tive, all models share the same vision encoder architecture, CLIP ViT-L/14 1, and are pretrained on the same dataset, varying only in the alignment objective used.

Local Alignment Objectives 

Local Captioning We adopt a set of regional captioning tasks to learn local correspondences between text and image regions. Regions are defined by anatomical structures in the chest, such as the mediastinum and left lung, and each is represented by a bounding box paired with a caption. From these annotations, we formulate three local vision-language tasks. Grounded Captioning (GCAP) generates a regional caption given an image and a bounding box. Automatic Re-ferring Expression (AREF) predicts a bounding box given an image and a regional caption. Conditional Automatic Re-ferring Expression (CAREF) generates both a caption and a bounding box given an image and the anatomical name of a region. We train the model on all three tasks jointly in a mul-titask setup (Beyer et al. 2023). Each task is formulated as an autoregressive sequence generation problem and optimized with the objective in Equation 3, with a task-specific prompt prepended to indicate the desired task and the loss computed over the generated tokens only. The decoder follows the same setup as global captioning. 

Local Contrastive We extend contrastive learning from the image-report level to the region-caption level. Instead of aligning entire chest X-ray images with reports, we align anatomically defined image regions with their corresponding phrases: for each phrase, we contrast the ViT patch tokens within its bounding-box region against the phrase embed-ding. Regions that share the same phrase text and anatom-ical name are treated as positive pairs, while all remaining region-phrase combinations are treated as negatives. We op-timize this objective with the same formulation, Transformer encoder, and training setup as global contrastive learning, differing only in the granularity of the aligned pairs. 

Combined Local and Global Objectives 

Local Contrastive Learning and Global Captioning Ashared vision encoder is trained jointly on both objectives, with separate task-specific heads: a bidirectional transformer encoder for regional contrastive learning and a Transformer decoder for global captioning. To balance the two objectives, training is interleaved so that each is optimized on alternating steps, giving both an equal number of updates. 

Local Captioning and Global Captioning To incorpo-rate global supervision with local captioning, we introduce captioning (CAP), which prompts the model to generate the full radiology report from the input image. All four tasks are trained jointly within a multitask autoregressive frame-work using a shared vision encoder and text decoder, with all four tasks sampled in equal proportion so that no single task dominates.

Local Captioning and Global Contrastive A shared vi-sion encoder is trained jointly on both local captioning and global contrastive objectives, with separate task-specific heads: a bidirectional encoder for global contrastive learning and a transformer decoder for local captioning. Training of the two objectives is interleaved to balance the contribution from the objectives. 

# Experiment 

Task Setup 

We use MIMIC-CXR (Johnson et al. 2019) for vision-language pretraining, retaining only PA and AP views, ex-cluding studies missing a findings or impression section, and removing references to prior imaging. After filtering, 242,575 studies remain across the official splits. We parti-tion the training set into two disjoint subsets: 100,000 studies for pretraining and 99,341 for downstream training of report generation, VQA, and medical difference VQA. Images are resized to 384 × 384 and converted to RGB. In all down-stream experiments, we freeze the encoder and train only a task-specific head, isolating the effect of the pretraining objective. For objectives requiring local supervision, we use Chest ImaGenome (Wu et al. 2021), which augments MIMIC-CXR with region-level bounding boxes and report phrases. Since identical phrases often map to multiple regions, we assign each phrase to a single anatomical structure, choosing the most specific region when nested regions apply; negative findings are assigned to the region where the pathology would typically manifest. Bounding boxes are represented as 

[xmin , y min , x max , y max ], normalized by image dimensions and scaled by 1000. All models use the CLIP ViT-L/14 (Radford et al. 2021) vision encoder, trained from scratch alongside a text module instantiated as a bidirectional Transformer encoder for con-trastive objectives or an autoregressive decoder for caption-ing objectives. Both text module variants use 6 layers, 8 atten-tion heads, hidden dimension 512, feed-forward dimension 2048, and dropout 0.1, with the GPT-2 tokenizer (50,257 to-kens), learned positional embeddings, and a learned [CLS] 

token for sequence-level representations. Models are trained for 50 epochs with AdamW, a learning rate of 1 × 10 −4, and cosine annealing; we select the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss. The resulting encoders are evaluated on six downstream tasks spanning the granularity spectrum introduced in the Introduction Section, each described in the following sub-sections. The performance for all generative tasks is mea-sured using BLEU-1/4, ROUGE-1/L, RadGraph-F1, and BERTScore. The performance for classification is measured using AUC while that of abnormality detection is mea-sured using mAP@0.4 and mAP@0.5. We report the mean and standard deviation over three seeds. We perform paired bootstrap resampling (Berg-Kirkpatrick, Burkett, and Klein 2012) with Benjaminin-Hochberg FDR correction (Ben-jamini and Hochberg 1995) across all pairwise comparisons; full results are presented in the technical supplement. 

Multilabel Classification Our primary evaluation proto-col for classification is linear probing. In this setting, a single linear layer is trained on top of visual representations ex-tracted from the vision encoder using binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss with SGD optimizer, a learning rate of 0.1, cosine annealing scheduler, a batch size of 512, and for 30 epochs. We utilize the NIH CXR-14 dataset ( ?), a 14-class multi-label dataset comprising 94,596 chest X-ray images from 30,805 patients. We strictly follow the official train and test splits. 

Abnormality Detection We train a DETR ( ?) detection head on the frozen features using a combination of classi-fication, L1 bounding-box regression, and generalized IoU losses:

It uses 20 learned object queries, a linear classification head over 15 classes (14 pathologies plus background), and a 3-layer MLP regression head. The decoder is trained on features from the 99,341 MIMIC-CXR training images excluded from the pretraining stage. The decoder architecture is described under task setup. Visual Question Answering We train a text decoder on the frozen vision features to answer questions about chest X-ray images, using the single-image subset of the Medical Difference VQA dataset (Hu et al. 2023a). The questions span six categories — location, presence, abnormality, type, severity, and view — with 202,982 training and 25,689 test questions. To avoid data leakage, we retain only questions whose images were excluded from pretraining. The decoder architecture is described under Task Setup: at each layer, masked self-attention models the question and previously generated answer tokens, while cross-attention integrates the visual features. It is trained from scratch with cross-entropy loss, using AdamW for 10 epochs with a learning rate of 

1 × 10 −4, batch size 64, and cosine annealing with a 5% linear warm-up. 

Medical Difference VQA We train a text decoder on the frozen vision features to describe longitudinal changes be-tween a pair of chest X-rays acquired from the same patient at different time points. We use the “difference” subset of the Medical Difference VQA dataset, comprising 104,066 train-ing, 12,934 validation, and 12,918 test questions. To prevent data leakage, we ensure that neither image in any test pair appears in the pretraining dataset. The frozen features from the two images are concatenated into a single cross-attention memory, with learned frame embeddings added to indicate whether each token originates from the reference or current image. In each decoder layer, masked self-attention models the question and previously generated answer tokens, while cross-attention integrates information from both images. The decoder architecture is described under Task Setup. It is trained from scratch with cross-entropy loss, using AdamW for 20 epochs with a learning rate of 1 × 10 −4, weight decay 0.01, batch size 20, and cosine annealing.

Pretraining granularity aligns with task granularity at the extremes 

The clearest support for the granularity hypothesis comes from the two tasks at opposite ends of the spatial granularity spectrum. On abnormality detection (VinDr-CXR, Figure 3), the most spatially demanding task, localized objectives out-perform global ones: Local Contrastive (6.56 mAP@0.4) and Local Captioning (5.64) surpass all global single objec-tives, the best being Global Sigmoid (5.02). Conversely, on classification (NIH-CXR-14, Figure 2), a global task, global objectives win: Global Contrastive leads among single ob-jectives (65.3 AUC), with Global Sigmoid (65.0) and Global Captioning (64.5) also surpassing the best local objective, Local Contrastive (63.5). This alignment, with local objec-tives winning on the localized task and global objectives on the global one, is consistent with the hypothesis that pre-training granularity should match task granularity. Two nu-ances stand out. On detection, the local advantage is strongest for Local Contrastive, while Local Captioning’s margin over Global Sigmoid is modest. Within the global objectives on detection, Global Sigmoid substantially outperforms Global Contrastive (2.69) and Global Captioning (3.82), suggesting its pairwise binary loss provides a finer-grained signal than InfoNCE even at the image level. Detection and classification thus offer complementary evidence for the hypothesis at the two extremes; the intermediate tasks, examined next, reveal a more complex picture.

This suggests that the benefits of local pre-training are more clearly reflected in surface-level text quality than in clinical entity accuracy. Two complementary explanations account for this compet-itiveness. First, local objectives may produce representations that subsume global semantics: by learning to attend to spe-cific regions and findings, the encoder develops rich patch-level features that, when aggregated, yield stronger image-level representations than objectives optimized solely at the global level. Second, report generation and VQA, though nominally global, require some local understanding to pro-duce clinically accurate outputs, since reports describe spe-cific findings at specific anatomical locations and questions may probe specific regions. Local pretraining may there-fore confer an implicit advantage even on global tasks by equipping the encoder with the fine-grained detail needed for accurate clinical description.

This interaction suggests that the relative merits of captioning and contrastive learning cannot be assessed independently of the spatial scale at which they are applied. Mixing captioning and contrastive objectives yields the strongest combinations 

On classification and in-distribution generation, combina-tions mixing captioning and contrastive are strongest. Local contrastive and global captioning leads on NLG metrics for MIMIC-CXR report generation (Table 1), VQA (Table 2), and medical difference VQA (Table 3), and its mirror image, Local captioning and global contrastive, is similarly strong, indicating that the benefit steps from mixing supervision types rather than the granularity assignment. On classifica-tion, the combinations with global contrastive learning lead with a considerable margin over single objective encoders underscoring the contrastive component’s importance for discriminative tasks. Local captioning and global caption-ing departs from this pattern: it is worst on classification and on MIMIC-CXR RadGraph, yet strongest among combina-tions on the zero-shot IU X-ray and CheXpert Plus report generation datasets, revealing that pure captioning supervi-sion generalizes best out-of-distribution even when it under performs in-distribution. 

Limitations 

Our findings are scoped to the frozen-encoder transfer setting and may not hold under full finetuning, which could alter the relative standing of the objectives. We evaluate a single archi-tecture (CLIP ViT-L/14) at one pretraining scale, and most intermediate tasks derive from MIMIC-CXR, limiting out-of-distribution evidence. Finally, the local objectives require dense region-level annotations (bounding boxes paired with phrases), which are costly to obtain and unavailable for most datasets and imaging modalities, constraining the settings in which localized pretraining is practical. 

# Conclusion 

We investigated how pretraining objectives affect five chest X-ray interpretation tasks spanning two levels of granularity. Our results partially support the hypothesis that granularity-matched pretraining helps: localized objectives lead on ab-normality detection and global objectives on classification. The picture is more complex on intermediate tasks, where local objectives are competitive on report generation and VQA, outperforming global objectives on NLG metrics while global captioning retains an edge on RadGraph-F1. The merits of captioning versus contrastive learning also de-pend on granularity, with global captioning beating global contrastive but local contrastive beating local captioning across most tasks. Among combinations, mixing caption-ing and contrastive supervision is strongest on classification and in-distribution generation, while two captioning objec-tives generalize best on zero-shot report generation. These findings challenge the assumption that pretraining granular-ity maps cleanly onto task requirements, pointing instead to the interaction of objective type, granularity, and task as the primary determinant of downstream performance.
