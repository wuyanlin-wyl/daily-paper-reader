Abstract 

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) requires frequent imaging-based assessment, yet interpretation of modalities such as wireless capsule endoscopy (WCE) and intestinal ultrasound remains heavily dependent on specialist expertise. Vision-Language Models (VLMs) demonstrate significant potential in multimodal medical image analysis, but their clinical adoption is hindered by their insufficient domain-specific reasoning, susceptibility to hallucination, scarcity of good quality training data in fine-grained diagnostics and limited interpretability. We introduce ARISE (Adaptive Agentic Reasoning with Image-grounded Self-Evaluation), an autonomous planning framework that models few-shot medical image understanding as a sequential agentic work-flow. ARISE structures agent execution into a transparent 5-stage workflow: hypothesis generation, image-grounded evidence summarization, evidence-conditioned refinement, symbolic verification, and final diagnosis. We apply ARISE to IBD assessment across two independent patient cohorts: wireless capsule endoscopy (WCE) images for Crohn’s disease and B-Mode ultrasound data for Ulcerative Colitis. ARISE consistently improves diagnostic performance over baseline VLMs while exposing where reasoning succeeds or fails, providing a more interpretable basis for clinical decision support and realistic deployment. 

# Introduction 

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including Crohn’s dis-ease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), is a chronic relapsing condition that requires repeated assessment of disease ac-tivity to prevent progressive bowel damage and other com-plications (Ng et al. 2017; Turner et al. 2021; Torres et al. 2017). Two imaging modalities are central to this monitoring workflow. Wireless capsule endoscopy (WCE) (Iddan et al. 2000) provides direct visualization of small-bowel mucosal abnormalities in Crohn’s disease, while intestinal ultrasound (IUS) (Maaser et al. 2020) offers a radiation-free means of assessing bowel-wall features such as colon wall thickening and loss of stratification, both established markers of activ-ity in ulcerative colitis (Bots et al. 2018). Despite their clin-ical value, both modalities share a critical bottleneck: accu-rate interpretation depends heavily on scarce, subspecialty-trained experts (Vall´ ee et al. 2020; Leenings et al. 2025). In resource-limited and non-tertiary settings, this dependence delays diagnosis and monitoring precisely where timely in-tervention matters most (Kaplan and Windsor 2021). Vision-language models (VLMs) offer a promising way to support medical image interpretation without task-specific training (Radford et al. 2021; Alayrac et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2023; Achiam et al. 2023). Yet their translation into IBD assessment is hindered by four well-documented obstacles (Hager et al. 2024). First, general-purpose VLMs exhibit a domain-reasoning gap: fine-grained IBD diagnosis requires integrating subtle, co-occurring visual cues over disease pro-gression rather than recognizing salient objects, and these cues are underrepresented in general training distributions (Du, Yu, and Wang 2025; Zhang et al. 2025). Second, VLMs remain prone to hallucination and to internally contradictory rationales, an unacceptable failure mode in a clinical setting (Ji et al. 2023; Li et al. 2025; Madaan et al. 2023). Third, high-quality annotated IBD imaging data are scarce, ruling out conventional fine-tuning in most practical deployments (Shakeri et al. 2024; Vall´ ee et al. 2020). Finally, even accu-rate VLMs act as black boxes, providing no way to distin-guish sound visual reasoning from chance or spurious short-cut (Byun et al. 2025, 2026); inference-time methods such as test-time prompt tuning and retrieval-augmented in-context learning avoid retraining, but do not necessarily produce rea-soning that can be inspected or verified(Shu et al. 2022; Ram et al. 2023; Zhan et al. 2026; Xia et al. 2025). Neurosymbolic methods that translate natural-language problems into symbolic form for solver-backed verification improve logical faithfulness, but they have been developed for text-only reasoning rather than image-grounded medical diagnosis, where hypotheses must be tied to observable vi-sual findings (Pan et al. 2023; Olausson et al. 2023; Garcez and Lamb 2023). Moreover, most medical-agent evaluations emphasize final-task accuracy, making it difficult to iden-tify where failures arise within a multi-stage reasoning pro-cess (Liu et al. 2024; Fu et al. 2025; Hager et al. 2024). As recent workflow-aware benchmarks have argued, final-output metrics alone cannot reveal where or why an agent fails (Schmidgall et al. 2024; Jiang et al. 2025). In short, no existing few-shot adaptation approach produces a reasoning state that is simultaneously grounded in the image evidence, verified for internal consistency, and open to expert inspec-tion before deployment. We introduce ARISE (Adaptive Agentic Reasoning with arXiv:2610.04777v1 [cs.CV] 3 Oct 2026 Image-grounded Self-Evaluation), an autonomous frame-work that adapts a frozen VLM to few-shot IBD assessment by constructing, grounding, verifying, and refining an ex-plicit set of diagnostic hypotheses, and that evaluates both its intermediate reasoning stages and its final diagnosis. ARISE follows five stages: (S1) generation of diagnostic hypothe-ses from task and clinical knowledge, (S2) image-grounded summarization of findings from a small support set, (S3) evidence-conditioned refinement of those hypotheses, (S4) symbolic verification and repair using first-order logic and an SMT solver, and (S5) final diagnosis of unseen im-ages. This design makes transparency and correctness mu-tually reinforcing, and constrains hallucination without fine-tuning. Each stage produces an inspectable artifact and an associated score, allowing ARISE to evaluate both the qual-ity of its own reasoning process and the accuracy of the re-sulting diagnosis evaluated in two settings: Wireless Capsule Endoscopy (WCE) for Crohn’s disease and B-mode ultra-sound for ulcerative colitis. Our contributions are fourfold: First, we introduce a few-shot adaptation strategy in which domain knowledge is rep-resented as image-grounded, iteratively refined hypotheses rather than learned parameters. Second, we extend solver-backed neurosymbolic reasoning to image-grounded med-ical diagnosis by verifying and repairing VLM-generated hypotheses before prediction. Third, we introduce stage-level evaluation that separates agentic process quality from task performance, revealing where reasoning failures oc-cur rather than reducing evaluation to a final label. Finally, we demonstrate the framework across independent WCE (Vall´ ee et al. 2020) and B-mode ultrasound (Leenings et al. 2025) cohorts and multiple VLM families, showing that structured, verifiable reasoning can improve diagnostic per-formance across modalities and model backbones. 

# Methodology 

We describe ARISE (Adaptive Agentic Reasoning with Image-grounded Self-Evaluation ), an autonomous agent that adapts a frozen vision-language model (VLM) to few-shot inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) assessment by executing a transparent, five-stage reasoning workflow. Unlike a single opaque prompt, ARISE carries an explicit workflow state 

across stages, invokes an external symbolic tool, and emits an inspectable artifact at every stage that receives its own 

score . No VLM parameters are updated: adaptation is stored entirely as an auditable hypothesis state. This eliminates the dependency on massive datasets, bypassing the conventional fine-tuning bottleneck that typically hinders medical AI de-ployment. We summarize a run as an agentic (process) score over the five workflow stages plus a task (outcome) score, and combine them into a single overall score.

# Experiments 

Datasets and Tasks 

ARISE is evaluated across two IBD imaging domains: Crohn’s disease (CD) using wireless capsule endoscopy (WCE) and ulcerative colitis (UC) using B-mode intestinal ultrasound. Detailed dataset composition, support/few-shot examples/test splits, and task definitions are provided in Ap-pendix B. For Crohn’s disease, we use the CrohnIPI dataset (Vall´ ee et al. 2020), a wireless capsule endoscopy (WCE) collec-tion of 3,498 RGB images independently reviewed by mul-tiple clinical experts. We evaluate three tasks of increasing diagnostic granularity: (1) Pathological vs non-pathological image classification, (2) ulceration diagnosis (ulceration vs. no ulceration), and (3) six-lesion classification for patholog-ical images, with labels being aphthoid ulceration, stenosis, edema, erythema, ulceration between 3 and 10 mm, and ul-ceration > 10 mm. For Ulcerative Colitis, ultrasound data is taken from the C-TRUS dataset (Leenings et al. 2025), containing a total of 800 ultrasound images collected from 13 patients. We evaluate colon-wall stratification status, classifying whether bowel-wall layering is preserved or lost, a clinically relevant marker of intestinal inflammation. 

Models and Prompting 

We evaluate ARISE using instruction-following VLMs from three model families: GPT, Gemini, and Qwen. We test GPT-5.4 (Afridi et al. 2026), GPT-4o-mini (Achiam et al. 2023), Gemini-2.5 (Comanici et al. 2025), and Qwen2-VL (Wang et al. 2024), with Qwen2-VL serving as an open-weight VLM baseline. All models are evaluated on identi-cal query splits. Automatic hypothesis generation and logi-cal premise construction use a reasoning-oriented language model, while symbolic contradiction detection is performed using the Z3 SMT solver (De Moura and Bjørner 2008). We perform 2 major experiments. 1. We compare the di-agnostic accuracy, F1 score, precision, and recall of the base VLM and the VLM with the final generated hypotheses (task score after stage 5). 2. We then compare the agentic scores for every stage across models, and the overall task score for every model, on the 6-lesion classification task. # Results 

Diagnostic Performance: Base VLM vs. ARISE 

Table 1 compares base-model accuracy with the same VLMs operating within the ARISE workflow across the three Crohn’s disease WCE tasks. ARISE improves accuracy for every model, with the largest accuracy gain observed as the task complexity increases (6-lesion classification being the hardest) and for the weaker baseline models. Qwen2-VL and Gemini-2.5 close much of the gap to the stronger GPT baselines once wrapped in the ARISE workflow, suggesting that the framework’s gains come from structuring and ver-ifying reasoning rather than from any one backbone’s raw visual capability. Detailed task-level results for this dataset, including accuracy, F1 score, precision, and recall for all model–task combinations, are provided in the Appendix. For the ulcerative-colitis cohort, Table 2 reports the corre-sponding accuracy, F1, precision, and recall for the B-mode ultrasound colon-wall-stratification task (preserved vs. lost). The ARISE framework results in a consistent improvement over all baseline models even when diagnosing from noisy B-mode ultrasound images, showing that the performance improvement generalizes across different tasks and modali-ties. Stage-Level Reasoning Quality 

Figure 3 reports the five individual stage scores (s1 through s5) for all four models on the six-lesion classification task, together with the mean across models at each stage. Hypoth-esis generation (S1) and symbolic verification (S4) are con-sistently strong across models, while evidence-guided re-finement (S3) is the most challenging stage and exhibits the greatest model variation. The contrast between the two kinds of stages points to a broader pattern: agents are consistently better at rule-based steps than at visually-grounded refine-ment, where they must revisit the image evidence itself and use it to revise their own hypotheses rather than follow a fixed procedure. The strongest models also perform better during refinement and final diagnosis, indicating that visu-ally grounded adaptation is a key factor separating stronger from weaker agents. 

Agentic and Task-level scores across models 

We examine how each model’s intermediate reasoning qual-ity (the agentic score) relates to its final classification perfor-mance (the task score) on the six-lesion classification task. Figure 4 reports the overall, agentic, and task scores for all four models, ranked by overall score. Across models, stronger agentic performance generally corresponds to stronger diagnostic performance, but the re-lationship is not one-to-one. The rule-based stages, such as hypothesis generation and symbolic verification, are con-sistently strong across all models, whereas the largest dif-ferences emerge during evidence-guided refinement and fi-nal diagnosis, where the model must use visual evidence to revise and apply its hypotheses. Hence weaker mod-els can maintain relatively high agentic scores by success-fully completing structured stages such as hypothesis gen-eration and verification, while still struggling to translate that reasoning into the correct final classification. The result-ing process–outcome gap highlights the value of ARISE’s stage-level evaluation, which reveals where reasoning qual-ity stops carrying through to diagnostic performance. 

Qualitative Visual Grounding 

To illustrate the mechanism behind these quantitative gains, Figure 5 presents a representative B-mode ultrasound case from the colon-wall-stratification task with ground-truth la-bel ”lost.” Queried directly, GPT-5.4 hedges between com-peting visual interpretations and concludes an incorrect diagnosis. Under the ARISE workflow, the same under-lying model grounds its answer in concrete, vocabulary-anchored observations (”the bowel wall appears diffusely hypoechoic and homogeneous, with no separable alternat-ing layers or consistent banding along the segment”) and correctly predicts ”lost.” This case is typical of the pattern underlying the quantitative improvements described ear-lier: ARISE’s evidence-summarization (S2) and symbolic-verification (S4) stages guide the model away from ambigu-ous, hedged visual claims and toward grounded and verifi-able ones, which is where the framework’s accuracy gains originate. 

# Path to Deployment 

ARISE is designed as a clinician-in-the-loop decision-support system rather than an autonomous diagnostic tool, emphasizing interpretability, error detection, and compati-bility with existing IBD imaging workflows. The current framework has been evaluated across two complementary IBD imaging settings: 3,498 wireless capsule endoscopy (WCE) images from the publicly available CrohnIPI dataset (Vall´ ee et al. 2020) and B-mode intestinal ultrasound data obtained from the C-TRUS dataset (Leenings et al. 2025) and annotated by an expert at the Indiana University (IU) School of Medicine. Clinical experts informed the visual cues, decision boundaries, and interpretation guidelines used by ARISE. Unlike black-box deep-learning based models, ARISE exposes the visual findings, diagnostic hypotheses, symbolic verification results, refinement steps, and final rec-ommendation, allowing clinicians to inspect or override rea-soning before acting on the output. The next deployment stage is retrospective clinical vali-dation using anonymized IU School of Medicine data from approximately 40 adults with Crohn’s disease, collected by a gastrointestinal ultrasound expert certified by the Inter-national Bowel Ultrasound Group (IBUS), an international organization supporting standardized training, research, and clinical adoption of intestinal ultrasound for IBD care. Ex-pert annotations of bowel-wall stratification and clinical assessments will serve as reference standards for evaluat-ing ARISE predictions. Stage-level errors will be analyzed across disease severity, patient variation, image quality, and acquisition hardware. For patients with anonymized, clinically indicated en-doscopy data, ARISE will also estimate the Simple En-doscopic Score for Crohn’s Disease (SES-CD) (Daperno et al. 2004), from AI-identified ulcer size, ulcerated surface, and luminal narrowing across the terminal ileum and four colonic segments. This will be compared to the SES-CD score assessments by experts. Following retrospective validation, ARISE will be incor-porated into a software-based clinical interface capable of processing endoscopy and B-mode ultrasound data streams. In an initial shadow-mode study, ARISE outputs will be compared with specialist interpretation without influencing patient care. A subsequent clinician-in-the-loop pilot will al-low specialists to accept, question, or override intermediate reasoning and final recommendations, providing direct evi-dence of where the system’s explanations are clinically use-ful or insufficient. Finally, nurses and generalist clinicians will participate in a small case study evaluating whether ARISE’s visual-cue explanations improve non-expert interpretation and diagnos-tic accuracy relative to unaided assessment (Figure 6). Suc-cessful prospective evaluation would support testing ARISE across multiple hospitals and integrating it with existing clinical imaging and reporting systems. This staged progres-sion, from retrospective validation, to silent real-time test-ing, to clinician-assisted evaluation and multi-site deploy-ment, provides a practical path toward safe, interpretable use of ARISE in IBD assessment. # Conclusion 

We presented ARISE, a five-stage agentic framework that adapts a frozen vision-language model to few-shot IBD as-sessment by generating, grounding, verifying, and refining diagnostic hypotheses without any fine-tuning. Across two independent cohorts (WCE for Crohn’s disease and B-mode ultrasound for ulcerative colitis) and three VLM families, ARISE improved accuracy, F1, precision, and recall over baselines on every model–task combination, with the largest gains on the hardest task and weakest baseline model, while its stage-level scoring exposed cases where strong interme-diate reasoning did not fully carry through to final accuracy, highlighting a gap a single outcome metric would hide. Be-yond raw accuracy, ARISE’s key contribution is auditabil-ity: every reasoning stage is stored as an inspectable ar-tifact rather than collapsed into an opaque prediction, di-rectly addressing the domain-reasoning gap, hallucination risk, data scarcity, and black-box behavior limiting clinical VLM adoption. Future work extends this toward improving the model with the agentic scores and deployment in real-world clinical settings. References

# B. Datasets and experimental protocol 

Crohn’s disease (Wireless Capsule Endoscopy) The CrohnIPI dataset (Vall´ ee et al. 2020) contains 3,498 expert-reviewed WCE RGB images. The manuscript reports 60.55% normal and 38.85% pathological images. Patholog-ical images include erythema, edema, aphthoid ulceration, ulceration between 3 and 10mm, ulceration greater than 10mm, and stenosis; some images contain co-occurring la-bels. 

Ulcerative colitis (B-Mode Ultrasound) The C-TRUS dataset (Leenings et al. 2025) contains 800 B-mode ultra-sound images from 13 patients. The study filters 275 good-quality images for the colon-wall stratification experiment. These raw datapoints were annotated for colon-wall stratifi-cation classification, and segmentation of the colon wall, by an expert at IU School of Medicine. Experimental Protocol 

1. Base VLM evaluation: Each backbone is first evaluated in a query-only setting on the held-out test split. 2. ARISE evaluation: The same backbone is evaluated on the same query split after the five-stage ARISE workflow constructs the verified hypothesis state H∗.3. Task-level comparison: Accuracy, F1 score, precision, and recall are reported for Base and ARISE on all four clinical tasks. 4. Stage-level analysis: Scores S1–S5 are compared across the four VLMs on the Crohn’s disease six-lesion classi-fication task. S3 is computed on a separate 50-example validation subset. 5. Process vs. outcome analysis: The weighted AGENTIC score is compared with the TASK score to identify cases in which a well-formed reasoning process does not fully translate to correct final classification.

# D. Prompt Templates and Implementation 

Prompt A: Normal vs. Pathological WCE Classification 

The binary prompt assigns each WCE frame to exactly one of two labels, N (normal/non-pathological) or P (patholog-ical). The prompt defines pathological findings to include aphthoid ulceration, ulceration, erythema, edema, stenosis, focal mucosal breaks, depressed lesions, white/yellow ul-cer bases, red halos, abnormal redness, swelling, or luminal narrowing. The decision rules instruct the model to choose 

N only when the mucosa appears continuous and no focal abnormality is visible, and to choose P whenever a definite abnormality is present. In the baseline setting, no refined hy-potheses or in-context examples are provided. In the ARISE setting, the refined hypothesis list is inserted under a Sup-porting rules field and the model is asked to return the pre-dicted class together with the ID of the supporting hypothe-sis when available.             

> Prompt A template:
> You are a medical assistant specialized in wireless capsule endoscopy image classification. Classify each WCE image into exactly one binary label:
> N= normal-appearing / non-pathological frame.
> P= pathological / abnormal frame.
> Supporting rules: [refined hypotheses, ARISE setting only]
> Decision rules: choose Nonly for normal, continuous mu-cosa without focal abnormality; choose Pif any visible ab-normality is present; do not output a lesion subtype.

Prompt B: Six-Lesion WCE Classification 

Prompt B is applied only to frames already considered pathological and assigns exactly one lesion label: aphthoid ulceration ( AU ), erythema ( E), edema ( O), stenosis ( S), ul-ceration 3–10 mm ( U3-10 ), or ulceration >10 mm ( U>10 ). The prompt includes a short description of each class and an ulcer-first decision hierarchy: visible mucosal break, de-pression, erosion, crater, or white/yellow ulcer base directs the model toward an ulcer subtype; intact-surface redness fa-vors erythema; swollen/enlarged villi with a continuous sur-face favors edema; and dominant luminal narrowing favors stenosis. The baseline Prompt B uses a fixed set of short class-discrimination rules. In the ARISE setting, these fixed rules are replaced by the refined hypothesis state loaded from disk.                

> Prompt B template:
> You are a medical assistant specialized in wireless capsule endoscopy image classification. This image is already considered pathological. Do not choose N.Classify the pathological subtype into exactly one of:
> AU, E, O, S, U3-10, U>10 .
> Supporting rules: [fixed short rules for baseline; refined hypotheses for ARISE]
> Decision hierarchy: ulcerative surface loss →ulcer sub-type; intact-surface redness →E; swollen/enlarged villi with continuous surface →O; dominant luminal narrowing
> →S.
