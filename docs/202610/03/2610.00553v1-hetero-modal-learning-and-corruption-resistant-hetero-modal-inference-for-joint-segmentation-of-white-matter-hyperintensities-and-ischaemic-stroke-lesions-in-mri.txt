Abstract 

White matter hyperintensities (WMH) and ischaemic stroke lesions (ISL) are visually confounding, co-occurring pathologies that require large, diverse datasets for robust deep learning segmentation. However, assembling such datasets is hindered by cohort samples that lack reference segmentations from both features and complete sets of MRI structural sequences (i.e., “modali-ties”). To maximise data utility, we investigate hetero-modal learning using a dataset of 206 vascular disease patients across four MRI sequences (T1-weighted, T2-weighted, fluid-attenuated inversion recovery, and diffusion-weighted imaging) with expert annotations of both WMH and ISL. We demonstrate that hetero-modal learning outperforms models trained using a single imaging modality in scenarios with substantial missing data, includ-ing a split where only 10% of the training data contains all four modalities while the remainder is uni-modal, and a split relying on a single shared “an-chor” modality with zero overlap between the remaining modalities. 1. Introduction 

White matter hyperintensities (WMH) and ischaemic stroke lesions (ISL) are pathological features often visible on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans of individuals with cerebral small vessel disease (SVD) (Duering et al., 2023), a vascular disease affecting the small blood vessels. Their automatic segmentation and differentiation is inherently challenging due to their sim-ilar visual appearance in most MRI sequences: both lesion types present as hypointense on T1-weighted (T1w) MRI sequences and hyperintense on T2-weighted (T2w) MRI sequences, including the fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequence. Although diffusion-weighted images (DWI) can sometimes distinguish ISL from WMH, this is typically only effective when ISL is acute and, as apparent diffusion coefficients vary with age, body temperature, scanning settings, and tissue specific characteristics, this MRI modality is not always a reliable way to identify ISL (Edlow et al., 2017). While visually differentiating these confounding lesions is difficult, it is a task well suited to deep learning, as neural networks excel at uncovering highly complex patterns. To achieve robust performance, however, these models must be trained on as much expertly annotated data as possible, and this data should ideally originate from a large variety of sources (i.e., imaging acquisition protocols and MRI scanners). Beyond a general scarcity of such data, model development is hindered by two main factors: 1) not all datasets with WMH or ISL annotations have them for both lesion types; and 2) clinical datasets rarely provide a complete, pristine set of MRI sequences: sequences are frequently missing, or present but severely degraded by artefacts. The 2first issue describes the problem of learning from partially labelled datasets, which has been previously explored (Phitidis et al., 2026). In this paper, we focus on the second issue, investigating the merits of hetero-modal learning, defined as learning from heterogeneous subsets of all unique MRI sequence types available throughout the training dataset. Whereas in clinical and medical physics the image acquisition method de-fines the imaging modality (e.g., computerised tomography, MRI, positron-emission tomography), in the field of computer science, it is becoming more common to refer to each distinct sequence type, even within MRI, as a modal-ity. In this manuscript we have adopted this definition, for compatibility with current literature in the field of medical image segmentation. A standard multi-modal segmentation model requires a fixed set of input modalities. A hetero-modal model, however, is designed to generate predic-tions from any available subset of imaging modalities. The seminal work in this area is HeMIS (Hetero-modal Medical Image Segmentation) by Havaei et al. (2016), which proposed the use of independent encoders to embed each modality into a shared latent space. Subsequent work (Dorent et al., 2019) incorporated variational autoencoders to reconstruct the missing modalities. More recently, convolutional neural networks (LeCun et al., 1989) have been combined with vision transformers (Vaswani et al., 2017) for more flexible hetero-modal processing (Zhang et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2025; Lan et al., 2025). Much of the literature focuses on hetero-modal inference , but hetero-modal learning is arguably the more important aspect of the framework. The primary advantage of training on a dataset where not all samples have a complete set of modalities is the ability to include a larger number of in-dividual subjects during training. This allows a model to capture a greater proportion of the anatomical and pathological variation present in the un-derlying clinical population. In theory, this also allows models to leverage “privileged information”—meaning that learning from combined modalities can ultimately improve the model’s single-modality performance by encour-aging richer, correlated feature representations. In practice, however, this depends heavily on the dataset and the specific clinical task, and modality-specialist baselines still frequently outperform hetero-modal methods when trained on the same set of subjects. 3The recent literature has begun to explore hetero-modal learning. Shi et al. (2024) simulated imbalanced, incomplete modality scenarios in the BraTS (Menze et al., 2014) and MyOPS (Li et al., 2023) datasets. Lan et al. (2025) studied incomplete hetero-modal learning under varying miss-ing rates using BraTS. Wagner et al. (2025) investigated federated learning in seven datasets comprising seven MRI sequences and five pathologies. Using a multi-channel U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015) with zero-imputation (pass-ing missing modalities into the network as channels of zeros), they showed their model could perform segmentation on unseen modality combinations. In particular, they used the WMH segmentation challenge dataset (Kuijf et al., 2019) for WMH segmentation (T1w and FLAIR), alongside the AT-LAS v2.0 (Liew et al., 2022) (T1w only) and ISLES (Hernandez Petzsche et al., 2022) (T1w, T2w, FLAIR, and DWI) datasets for ISL segmentation. However, all pathologies were merged into a single “lesion” class. While the WMH Challenge, ATLAS v2.0, and ISLES datasets contain ground truth segmentations for WMH or ISL independently, no publicly available dataset currently provides joint ground truth annotations for both pathologies. This lack of joint labels restricts a model’s ability to accurately penalise false negatives when these visually similar lesions co-occur. Furthermore, mild SVD-related ISL (i.e., lacunar strokes) are under-represented in the ATLAS and ISLES cohorts. To address this gap, we leverage a privately held dataset containing expertly annotated ground truth segmentations for both WMH and ISL, with four distinct MRI sequences available (T1w, T2w, FLAIR, and DWI). While utilising this unique dataset allows us to rigorously evaluate hetero-modal learning for joint segmentation of WMH and ISL, real-world clinical deployment presents an additional hurdle: silent data corruption. A lim-itation of current hetero-modal approaches is the assumption that missing modalities are always correctly identified by the system. In automated im-age processing workflows, this is frequently not the case. MRI files might be filled with zeros or other constant voxel values, or a scan might be cor-rupted by artefacts severe enough to make it unusable. To overcome this, we introduce an additional layer of extreme data augmentation which we refer to as “corruption augmentation”. With this corruption augmentation in mind, we develop the Multimodal Attention Router (MMAR) block. While we do not comprehensively benchmark our MMAR-based model against the entirety of the hetero-modal literature, we present it as a proof-of-concept 4for a practical architectural solution designed to train corruption-robustness directly into the hetero-modal segmentation pipeline without diluting the representational capacity of the modality-specific encoders. This paper makes the following contributions: 1. We establish the first benchmark for joint hetero-modal segmentation of co-occurring WMH and ISL by leveraging a dataset of patients with subacute and chronic ISL and various degrees of vascular pathology, with ground truth annotations for both pathologies, and four MRI sequences. 2. We show that hetero-modal learning can train significantly better mod-els than fixed-modality baselines under a training data distribution containing only a small (10%) full-modality subset, with the remainder being strictly uni-modal. 3. We demonstrate that hetero-modal learning can produce models capa-ble of performing inference on unseen combinations of modalities. 4. To address the vulnerability of hetero-modal networks to silent data degradation, we introduce the Multimodal Attention Router (MMAR) block, an architectural solution designed to for dynamic feature routing, ensuring robustness against corrupted inputs. 5. We show that when trained with a “corruption augmentation” strategy, our MMAR-based model maintains strong performance in the face of catastrophic noise, severe bias field corruption, and modalities incor-rectly identified as being present. 

2. Experimental setup 

2.1. Dataset and processing 

We performed our experiments on the Mild Stroke Study 3 dataset (Clancy et al., 2021)—a prospective observational cohort study of patients presenting with clinically evident stroke syndromes, which was granted ethical approval by Southeast Scotland Regional Ethics Committee (ref. 18/SS/0044). T1w (3D protocol, 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm voxels), T2w (3D protocol, 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9

mm voxels), FLAIR (3D protocol, 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm voxels), and DWI (2D protocol, 2.0 × 2.0 × 2.0 mm voxels) MRI scans were acquired using a 3 Tesla Siemens Prisma MRI scanner. An expert manually delineated ISL masks, and WMH masks were produced via a semi-automatic approach and subse-quently corrected by an expert (Valdés Hernández et al., 2023). We selected 5the subset of 206 patients (mean age ± std = 66 ± 11; 33% female; 69% hypertensive; 21% diabetics) with a complete set of MRI sequences avail-able, in addition to WMH and ISL segmentation masks. Stroke sub-types included cortical and lacunar strokes affecting several locations including: middle cerebral artery, anterior cerebral artery, brain stem, cerebellum, in-ternal and external capsules, lentiform nucleus, and the thalamus. Prior to model training, all scans underwent skull-stripping using Synth-Strip (Hoopes et al., 2022), N4 bias-field correction (Tustison et al., 2010), and resampling to a 1 mm isotropic resolution. Data was randomly parti-tioned into 103 training, 41 validation, and 62 test subjects (lesion distribu-tions shown in Figure 1 ).

In this highly uni-modal setup, a complete set of sequences is available for only a small subset (10%) of the data, while the remaining subjects contribute only a single sequence. 2.2.3. Training split 3 

In this setup, we designated T1w as the “anchor sequence” due to its high prevalence in standard MRI protocols. All other sequences overlap with this T1w anchor, but never with each other. We used this distribution to investigate whether hetero-modal learning can train models capable of per-forming inference on novel, unseen combinations of sequences (e.g., FLAIR and DWI). 72.3. Checkpoint selection 

Because our primary objective is to evaluate the benefits of hetero-modal 

learning (i.e., maximising training data) rather than hetero-modal inference (i.e., using a single set of weights for all possible inputs), we evaluated the model using the checkpoint that achieved the highest validation Dice Simi-larity Coefficient (DSC) for each specific inference modality setup. In other words, for each of the hetero-modal learning methods, we store 15 different model checkpoints from a single training run. This ensures the fairest pos-sible comparison against the modality specialists, which naturally use their own optimal DSC checkpoints. However, for the corruption augmentation experiments, we assume that corrupted modalities cannot be identified a priori (otherwise, they would simply be treated as missing). Consequently, for those specific tests, we used the final model checkpoints—representing the state where the models have converged to a stable local minimum as the learning rate decays to zero. 

3. Methods 

We implemented two established baseline methods for hetero-modal seg-mentation: a multi-channel U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015) and U-HeMIS (Havaei et al., 2016). We also developed and evaluated our own archi-tecture, the Multimodal Attention Router (MMAR), designed to enhance model robustness against corrupted modalities. We compared these hetero-modal methods to modality specialist baselines. This section details each of these architectures and our training methodologies. Code is available at: 

https://github.com/Jesse-Phitidis/MultiModal .

3.1. Modality specialist baselines 

We trained modality-specialist baselines to serve as comparative bench-marks for the hetero-modal approaches. Each specialist model is trained and evaluated on a fixed, unique, modality combination. Consequently, these models cannot leverage the full multi-modal training data present in splits 2 and 3 (with the exception of the T1w specialist in training split 3). To prevent overfitting on their simpler learning tasks, the specialist baselines were configured with a standard 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, and 320 channels per resolution level, totalling approximately 16.7M parameters. 83.2. Multi-channel U-Net 

A multi-channel U-Net serves as the most straightforward hetero-modal method. Missing modalities are handled via zero-imputation (passed as chan-nels of zeros), requiring the network to handle extreme distribution shifts within its very first layers. To ensure a fair comparison and provide the model with sufficient capacity to learn robustness to missing channels, we matched the parameter counts of the other hetero-modal methods (91M pa-rameters) by configuring a large U-Net with 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, and 1024 channels at each respective resolution level. 

3.3. U-HeMIS 

As introduced in Section 1, HeMIS utilises an independent convolutional encoder for each modality. U-HeMIS extends this logic to the skip connec-tions at every level of a U-Net architecture. At each resolution level, the network calculates the mean and variance across the feature maps extracted from the available modalities. These statistical representations are then con-catenated along the channel dimension and processed by a single, shared convolutional decoder. For our experiments, we configured each independent encoder with 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, and 512 channels. 

3.4. Multimodal Attention Router (MMAR) 

Motivated by the challenge presented by corrupted modalities which are not marked as missing, we propose the Multimodal Attention Router (MMAR) block. Our method, shown in Figure 3 , is inspired by U-HeMIS and SFusion (Liu et al., 2023). 

3.4.1. Overview 

Our MMAR-based model replaces the statistical feature aggregation of U-HeMIS with a learnable dynamic feature router over the spatial and channel dimensions. The output is a single fused feature representation, with the important channels and spatial locations of each modality amplified, while irrelevant features are suppressed. This allows the MMAR to learn to down-weight features generated from corrupted modalities.

3.5. Implementation and training details 3.5.1. 3D U-Net architecture 

All methods shared a common 3D U-Net backbone based on nnU-Net (Isensee et al., 2021), using two Conv-Norm-Act blocks per resolution level across six levels. We used 3 × 3 × 3 convolutions, instance normalisation with affine scaling, and Leaky ReLU activations with a slope of 0.01. The encoder blocks incorporated residual connections, using strided convolutions for downsampling. Decoder upsampling was achieved via 2×2×2 transposed convolutions (stride 2). 

3.5.2. Modality dropout 

To train the hetero-modal methods, we applied random modality dropout. For each training sample with T available modalities (where T may already be less than the total unique dataset modalities, N ), we sampled an integer 

D from the range [0, T − 1]. We randomly dropped D modalities, bringing the total number of missing modalities to M = ( N − T ) + D.

3.5.3. Standard data augmentation 

We applied standard nnU-Net-style spatial and intensity augmentations: random affine transforms (prob=0.2, isotropic scaling in [0.7, 1.4], rotation in [-90 ◦, 90 ◦]); Gaussian noise (prob=0.15, std in [0, 0.316]); Gaussian blur (prob=0.1, sigma in [0.5, 1.5]); contrast adjustment (prob=0.15, multiplier in [0.65, 1.5]); gamma augmentation (prob=0.15, gamma in [0.7, 1.5]); low-resolution simulation (prob=0.25 per modality, factor in [1, 2]); and random axis flipping (prob=0.5). We also simulated motion, ghosting, spike, and bias field MRI artefacts (prob=0.05 each, via TorchIO (Pérez-García et al., 2021)). Post-augmentation, images were rescaled to [0, 1], saturating at the 0.5 and 99.5 percentiles. 12 3.5.4. Corruption augmentation 

To equip the hetero-modal models with resilience to unidentified cor-rupted modalities, we performed some of our experiments with an additional layer of extreme data augmentation severe enough to render a scan almost or completely unusable. During training for these experiments, we reassigned (with 50% probability per scan) some of the D scans that were initially se-lected to undergo modality dropout, to undergo corruption instead. For each corrupted scan, the corruption transform was randomly selected as one of the following: gaussian noise (std=3); bias field (polynomial degree 3, coefficients in [0.4, 0.9]); low contrast (multiplier in [0, 0.1]); high contrast (multiplier in [10, 30]); Gaussian blur (sigma in [2, 6] per axis); Gibbs noise (alpha in [0.75, 1]); K-Space spike (intensity in [15, 18]); Rician noise (std=1). We randomly performed each of these corruptions on the entire scan, or on ran-dom patches of the scan (3-10 patches, each of size 40-80 voxels in each axis). We implemented these transformations using the MONAI library (Cardoso et al., 2022). Figure 4 shows examples.

4.2. Hetero-modal models win when trained on highly uni-modal data 

Table 2 highlights the value of hetero-modal learning for maximising training data utilisation when dealing with mostly non-overlapping MRI se-quences. Across almost all modality setups, the hetero-modal approaches outperformed the specialist models, except MMAR on T1w and U-HeMIS on DWI. The mean macro-average result was significantly better for all hetero-modal models. Furthermore, in every scenario involving more than one in-ference modality, all hetero-modal approaches yielded statistically significant improvements over the specialist U-Nets. In contrast to the complete-data scenario of split 1, the multi-channel U-Net generally outperformed both U-HeMIS and MMAR in split 2, though the mean differences were not statistically significant. The multi-channel U-Net’s advantage stems primarily from its strong ISL segmentation results, achieving the highest DSC across all inference combinations. Meanwhile, the MMAR method achieved the best WMH DSC, significantly outperforming U-HeMIS, but not the multi-channel U-Net. We hypothesise that this dynamic occurs because the independent en-coders of U-HeMIS and MMAR are exposed to less overall pathological vari-ation, which is a critical factor for successful ISL segmentation. In this highly uni-modal data split, the benefit of sharing features across the entire multi-channel U-Net appears to outweigh any drawbacks of early fusion via zero-imputation. 4.4. MMAR outperforms established hetero-modal models on corrupted data 

The final experiments evaluated model robustness against severe data degradation. We applied corruption augmentation during training on split 1 and tested under four scenarios: completely missing modalities, “false present” modalities (missing but unidentified as such), catastrophic Gaus-sian noise, and severe bias fields. As shown in Table 4 , when predicting with explicitly missing modal-ities, the multi-channel U-Net and MMAR performed slightly worse than they did without corruption augmentation, whereas U-HeMIS showed slight improvement. However, the results shift under the remaining three corruption scenarios (Tables 5, 6, and 7 ). Faced with false presents, Gaussian noise, or severe bias fields, the MMAR consistently outperformed both the multi-channel U-Net and U-HeMIS for both WMH and ISL segmentation, and exhibited minimal performance degradation when transitioning from explicitly missing to corrupted modality testing. We attribute the MMAR’s success to its router-based architecture and auxiliary router loss. During training, we observed the router loss drop rapidly and remain low. This mechanism drastically reduces the gradient flow to any encoder processing a corrupted image, protecting that encoder from wasting learning capacity on becoming robust to catastrophic noise.

The first row illustrates the baseline output when all modalities are clean and present. Here, the router is highly selective about where it assigns high attention weights to the DWI scan. The two prominent bright areas strongly align with the locations of the ISL, while weights remain comparatively sup-pressed outside these regions. This aligns with clinical intuition: DWI is a powerful modality for identifying acute ISL (often termed “DWI-positive”) but generally lacks anatomical detail and is acquired at a lower resolution. When FLAIR is completely missing, the attention distributions shift significantly—most notably for T2w and DWI. Features previously domi-nated by the FLAIR sequence in the fused representation are drawn from the remaining available modalities. Specifically, the network relies more heavily on the DWI encoder for grey matter features and the T2w encoder for back-ground features. When the FLAIR scan is subjected to catastrophic corruption (Gaussian noise, false present), the router assigns it negligible weight. The resulting attention maps for T1w, T2w, and DWI closely resemble those of the explic-itly missing FLAIR scenario. This demonstrates that the MMAR naturally learns to treat severely corrupted modalities as if they were missing. Under bias field corruption, the router leverages its spatial adaptivity to selectively allow FLAIR features to pass into the hetero-modal fusion only in regions where the scan remains less affected by the artefact.

28 5. Conclusion 

This study establishes the first benchmark for the joint hetero-modal segmentation of co-occurring white matter hyperintensities (WMH) and is-chaemic stroke lesions (ISL). By leveraging a unique, fully annotated, multi-sequence dataset, we demonstrated that hetero-modal learning effectively overcomes the rigid data requirements of traditional modality specialist mod-els. Rather than discarding incomplete scans, this approach maximises the utility of all available data. We showed this to be effective in data-constrained scenarios, with hetero-modal models generally outperforming modality spe-cialist baselines. Beyond accommodating missing data during training, our findings high-light the adaptability of hetero-modal networks at inference. When trained on disjointed datasets sharing only a single anchor modality, these models successfully generalised to novel modality combinations, maintaining robust predictive performance even when the highly informative FLAIR sequence was unavailable. Additionally, we address a major hurdle in clinical deployment: silent data degradation. By introducing the Multimodal Attention Router (MMAR) block alongside a complimentary corruption augmentation strategy, we showed that this proof-of-concept method can dynamically adapt to unflagged, catas-trophically corrupted modalities. Through spatially adaptive feature routing, the MMAR-based model learns to treat severely degraded regions in scans as missing, preserving segmentation accuracy in the presence of extreme noise, bias fields, and false-present modalities. Ultimately, these strategies offer a robust and practical pathway for de-veloping and deploying medical image segmentation tools in imperfect, real-world clinical environments. 

Limitations. We acknowledge several limitations in this study. First, it is likely that our comparative results are highly sensitive to the total volume and modality distribution of the training data; we urge caution when extrap-olating our results to other data distributions. Second, due to the number of individual training runs required and limited computational resources, our evaluations are centred on a single random training, validation, and test split, and each model was trained only once. While we attempted to mitigate the impact of these factors by performing statistical significance tests, we must highlight this limitation. Third, our evaluation relies solely on the Dice simi-larity coefficient (DSC). Although DSC has known limitations, it remains the 29 standard segmentation metric and correlates strongly with other measures. Since our primary objective was the relative comparison of methods rather than establishing absolute task performance, limiting our evaluation to DSC allowed us to reduce the complexity of the results while preserving statis-tical power. Fourth, due to a lack of truly corrupted scans in our dataset, test time corruption transformations were required for evaluating the models that were trained for corruption-robustness, using corruption augmentation. To mitigate this bias, we applied different transformation parameters during training and testing augmentations. Finally, because our primary motivation was to evaluate the viability of hetero-modal learning in data-starved clini-cal scenarios, we opted for foundational, proven baseline methods. However, an abundance of complex architectures and training techniques have been proposed for imbalanced hetero-modal learning—typically evaluated on the BraTS brain tumour dataset (Menze et al., 2014). We leave a systematic benchmarking of these methods applied to WMH and ISL segmentation for future work.
