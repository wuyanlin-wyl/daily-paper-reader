Abstract 

Understanding long videos requires fine-grained perception and multi-step, higher-order reasoning over complex, long-range spatio-temporal dynamics. Vision-language models (VLMs) encode video frames into visual tokens and attempt to perform both perception and multi-step planning latently, within a single for-ward pass. This coupled formulation, however, is bottlenecked by the LLM’s limited capacity to discover and execute multi-step strategies in its latent representations. To address this bottle-neck, we propose Hierarchical Programmatic Probing (HPP), a framework that decouples semantic perception from higher-order tem-poral reasoning by reformulating long video understanding as iterative, programmatic ex-ploration of a hierarchically segmented video. Specifically, a coding-capable LLM plans and executes a multi-step strategy in an interac-tive coding environment, probing the video for information and invoking a VLM for lo-calized perception on demand. To make prob-ing tractable over long videos, we introduce three components: information-density-aware hierarchical segmentation, late-interaction se-mantic retrieval, and structured probing func-tions for coarse-to-fine temporal localization. We validate HPP on LongVideoBench, which requires both fine-grained perception and long-range relational reasoning, and show that de-coupling the two via iterative programmatic probing yields substantial gains. Further re-sults on EgoSchema, VideoMME, and MLVU demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach across diverse long-video benchmarks. Rele-vant code and supporting materials are avail-able at: https:awaisrauf.com/hpp. 

1 Introduction 

Long video understanding has become increasingly critical as video content accounts for a growing share of global internet traffic across diverse do-mains. The dominant paradigm extends Large Language Models (LLMs) to video by projecting frames into the LLM’s embedding space via a vi-sual encoder and performing perception and reason-ing jointly over the resulting token sequence within a single forward pass (Zhu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023a; Liu et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2023a; Bai et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2025a). This monolithic formulation of VLMs is struc-turally mismatched for long video understanding. Unlike short clips, long videos require both local-ized perceptual grounding to extract sparse visual information from thousands of redundant frames and multi-step planning over long temporal hori-zons to reason about complex spatio-temporal in-teractions. However, recent work has shown that latent reasoning within a single forward pass is in-effective for resolving temporal dependencies and executing multi-step planning (Li et al., 2025c; Xu et al., 2026). Compounding this, the number of vi-sual input tokens grows rapidly with duration (e.g., one hour video produces 1.08M visual tokens in Gemini (Google, 2026)), inflating compute and KV-cache as LLMs scale while diluting relevant signal across the extended context (Liu et al., 2024). Video-agent frameworks aim to address these challenges by treating videos as text-like docu-ments, relying on dense captioning of sampled frames (Wang et al., 2023c; Zhang et al., 2024b; Kahatapitiya et al., 2025) or incorporating rigid search processes and external tools into prede-fined workflows (Wang et al., 2024b, 2025c; Yang et al., 2025c). For example, (Kahatapitiya et al., 2025) converts video chunks into a language repos-itory through captioning and summarization, en-abling downstream reasoning with LLMs, while VideoTree (Wang et al., 2025c) does dense cap-tioning of a tree structured video frames, inputting to LLM for reasoning in a single step. This re-liance on dense captioning and manually designed heuristics imposes rigid workflows that constrain adaptive exploration, limiting the ability to fully 1

LLMs have become significantly better at coding and structured problem-solving, ex-hibiting strong planning, multi-step execution, and emergent symbolic reasoning (Anthropic, 2025; Schroeder et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025b; Zhang et al., 2025a). These abilities enable them to au-tonomously decompose tasks and execute iterative strategies through code, making them particularly well-suited for long-horizon, programmatic reason-ing. Building on these programmatic and multi-step planning capabilities, we ask: can long-video un-derstanding be reformulated by decoupling seman-tic perception from higher-order temporal reason-ing, allowing a coding-capable LLM to plan and execute structured exploration while a VLMs han-dles event recognition and fine-grained perceptual extraction within frames or short segments? To this end, we cast long video understanding as itera-tive, programmatic probing over structured video segmentation. We propose Hierarchical Program-matic Probing for long video understanding (HPP), a framework that enables a coding-capable LLM to perform high-level reasoning while interactively exploring long videos within a coding environment. Within this framework, the LLM is responsible for strategy formulation, planning, and relational infer-ence through programmatic interaction, whereas a lightweight VLM is invoked for targeted semantic perception over selected frames or short segments. Video navigation through code, however, presents unique challenges compared to text: videos consist of massive, highly redundant frame sequences with varying information density, mak-ing naive frame-level processing computationally prohibitive. To enable efficient exploration, our framework provides the LLM with a set of struc-tured probing functions inside the programming environment. Specifically, we introduce (1) an information-density-aware, computationally effi-cient hierarchical structuring method that converts raw frames into multi-scale segments by exploit-ing temporal redundancy; (2) a motion-adaptive late-interaction semantic retrieval module for local-izing query-relevant segments; (3) structure-aware video sampling functions for fine-grained tempo-ral navigation; and (4) a small VLM for targeted spatio-temporal understanding of segments. To demonstrate the effectiveness of HPP, we first evaluate our approach on LongVideoBench (Wu et al., 2024), a large-scale long-video benchmark that stratifies videos by duration and task complex-ity across perception and relational reasoning. Con-sistent with our central hypothesis, decoupling se-mantic perception from programmatic reasoning enables strong long-horizon performance even with a smaller VLM. We further validate our approach on general long video understanding benchmarks, including EgoSchema, VideoMME, MLVU (Man-galam et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024), confirming the effectiveness of our proposed HPP across diverse long-video settings. 

2 Related Work 

Here, we briefly position our work in relation to prior research; a more detailed discussion is pro-vided in Appendix D. 

Long Video Understanding. Prior post-training works for long video understanding span a wide range of methods, including extending context to accommodate more visual tokens (Zhang et al., 2024c) and compressing visual tokens (Shen et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2025; Tao et al., 2025), often trading fine-grained details for efficiency. More relevant to our work, agent-based frameworks address long-context limitations through tool-augmented reasoning or predefined search procedures (Chen et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025c; Yang et al., 2025c), but typically rely on dense captioning, rigid heuristics, or multiple co-ordinated VLMs. In contrast, our method decou-ples perception from reasoning, reformulating long-video understanding as hierarchical programmatic probing in which an LLM navigates the video through code and adaptively discovers strategies for complex, long-horizon tasks. 

Coding-based Approaches for Vision Tasks. 

Neural Module Networks (NMNs) (Andreas et al., 2016; Hu et al., 2017; Johnson et al., 2017; Hu et al., 2018) pioneered compositional visual ques-tion answering, decomposing each question into a program layout that is then executed over the im-age. However, these approaches require learning program layouts. More recent methods have used LLM’s code generation abilities to solve image un-derstanding tasks in single forward pass(Surís et al., 2023; Gupta and Kembhavi, 2023; Marsili et al., 2025). 3 Method 

Our method consist of an LLM navigating video through code execution and a set of efficient video probing functions. To fully exploit the LLM’s reasoning and coding abilities without imposing a rigid task formulation, we initialize the LLM with minimal inputs: a system prompt for context, video metadata, and the user query. The model is given access to (i) an interactive Python envi-ronment (REPL) initalized with system variables, (ii) a set of video probing functions for structured navigation, and (iii) a small vision-language model (VLM) for targeted spatio-temporal understanding. Given a query, the reasoning-and-navigation LLM first interprets the task and formulates a high-level strategy. It then executes this strategy by writing and executing Python code, selectively in-voking probing functions to navigate the video and querying the VLM when fine-grained visual under-standing is required. This iterative loop of reason-ing, code execution, and video interaction enables explicit symbolic reasoning while efficiently ex-ploring long videos, as illustrated in Figure 1. 

Interactive Hierarchical Programmatic Probing Environment 

The core of our framework is an interactive probing environment that enables a coding-capable LLM to iteratively navigate long videos, retrieve query-relevant evidence, and refine its reasoning based on intermediate execution feedback. Inspired by Recursive Language Models (Zhang et al., 2025a), we adopt a REPL-based interaction paradigm in which the LLM executes code within an isolated container and receives structured, terminal-style outputs, forming a tight reasoning–execution loop. Unlike natural language, videos consist of dense and highly redundant frame sequences, making naive frame-level exploration computationally pro-hibitive. To support efficient and structured prob-ing, we design four key modules within the REPL environment. First, we introduce a computation-ally efficient hierarchical structuring mechanism that leverages codec information to transform raw videos into multi-scale segments. Second, we design a semantic retrieval module that allows the LLM to localize query-relevant segments us-ing both global embeddings and fine-grained to-ken–frame late-interaction similarity. Third, we expose structured video sampling functions that enable fine-grained temporal navigation while con-straining exploration to a controlled action space. Finally, we integrate a lightweight VLM that can be queried for targeted spatio-temporal understanding over selected frames or clips, while higher-level reasoning remains within the LLM.

2) Fine-grained , Motion-aware Semantic Search for Query-based Localization. After di-viding a video into manageable segments, the next step is to enable the LLM to retrieve query-relevant content via text. Video retrieval methods typically employ a bi-encoder architecture, in which frames are uniformly sampled from a video segment, en-coded using a vision encoder, and aggregated into a single global embedding (Shu et al., 2024a; Xu et al., 2021). This global representation, together with a text embedding, is then used to compute similarity and retrieve relevant results. However, unlike general video retrieval, text-based localization within a single video requires a more fine-grained approach, as different segments may share substantial visual similarity. For ex-ample, localizing a segment corresponding to the query “bird flying over water” in a nature docu-mentary requires distinguishing it from visually similar scenes of birds flying over a desert. We provide a set of structured functions that support multi-scale temporal sampling. These functions include retrieving a frame at a specific timestamp, extracting frames over a time interval, hierarchy-aware frame sampling, temporal zoom-in within a selected segment, and segment clipping. 4 Effectiveness of Decoupled Perception and Reasoning

While L1 questions primarily test perceptual grounding such as recognizing objects, actions, and events within local segments, L2 demands relational rea-soning over temporally distributed evidence, requir-ing both fine-grained perception and long-horizon multi-step reasoning across long videos . How Code-based Programmatic Probing Works. 

Figure 4 illustrates HPP on a 4-scene temporal or-dering question from a 20-minute history documen-tary, where reused visual templates (e.g., colored borders) create many partial matches across 169 scenes. The LLM decomposes the question into four sub-goals, issues parallel semantic searches, zooms into candidates for VLM verification, and iteratively refines failed matches with varied query phrasings and cross-modal cues. After confirming all four scenes, it reasons over their temporal order to select the correct answer, inspecting only 68 of 33,909 frames (0.2%).

6 Conclusion 

We presented HPP, a programmatic framework for long-form video understanding that explicitly decouples semantic perception from higher-order temporal reasoning. Instead of treating videos as having uniformly distributed information, our ap-proach reformulates video understanding as itera-tive code-driven probing over hierarchically struc-tured video representations within a coding envi-ronment. In this setting, a coding-capable LLM performs planning, strategy execution, and rela-tional reasoning, while a lightweight VLM is in-voked only for targeted perceptual understanding. This design enables efficient exploration of long videos and better leverages the emerging reasoning capabilities of modern LLMs. 

7 Limitations 

While HPP is effective across a range of long-video benchmarks, several limitations remain. Firstly, HPP’s effectiveness is fundamentally tied to the coding LLM that drives probing. Our scaling ex-periments (Table 1) show that performance scales monotonically with the LLM’s coding ability: HPP inherits future gains from stronger LLMs but is also bounded by the LLM’s current capacity to decompose queries, formulate adaptive strategies, and recover from failed probes. Although externalizing reasoning into code cir-cumvents the depth limit of latent multi-step reason-ing, each individual probing decision still relies on the LLM’s intrinsic planning ability within a single turn. Weaker LLMs may issue ill-formed retrieval queries, terminate prematurely without sufficient evidence, or fail to chain probes coherently. More subtly, HPP also inherits the LLM’s biases: query interpretation, the choice of paraphrases for seman-tic retrieval, and the prompts issued to the VLM all reflect the LLM’s pretraining priors, which can systematically privilege some types of evidence or framings over others. Another limitation arises from HPP’s au-tonomous, multi-turn nature. Because the LLM plans and executes its own probing strategy, tra-jectories occasionally go awry in ways that are difficult to anticipate from the question alone. For instance, the model may latch onto an incorrect hy-pothesis early and confirm it through biased follow-up probes, repeatedly issue near-duplicate retrieval queries, or commit to a final answer before suf-ficient evidence has been gathered. Another fail-ure mode observed in with some smaller LLMs is model’s inability to return final answer and instead keep looping for more and more confirmations. A related failure mode is context filling: across multiple turns, intermediate reasoning, VLM re-sponses and especially verbose code outputs accu-mulate in the context window, and we occasion-ally observe the LLM weighting earlier (some-times weakly supported) evidence over more re-cent and more reliable findings, or losing track of the original query under accumulated con-text. These failures are characteristic of au-tonomous agentic systems and are not fully mit-igated by the structured probing API. Finally, our evaluation focuses on multiple-choice long-video QA benchmarks (LongVideoBench, EgoSchema, VideoMME, MLVU). Performance on open-ended generation, dense captioning, fine-grained tempo-ral grounding, or interactive video tasks remains to be characterized.

Segment-level Perception with Small VLM. To perform localized spatio–temporal understand-ing over the extracted segments, we employ a lightweight VLM (e.g., Qwen3VL-2B (Bai et al., 2023)) as a perception module. The LLM gener-ates task-conditioned textual prompts and submits them, together with the sampled frame(s) or short video clip, to the VLM in order to extract query-relevant visual and temporal evidence. The VLM is invoked without additional system prompts or ex-ternal contextual information; it functions purely as a visual–temporal encoder and interpreter. Higher-level reasoning, cross-segment aggregation, and decision-making remain entirely within the LLM. 

REPL Environment and Structured Probing Functions HPP exposes the video to the coding LLM through a stateful Python Read–Evaluate– Print Loop (REPL), in which the LLM emits code, observes printed output, and continues reasoning across turns. Each session is initialized with a small set of pre-loaded variables that anchor the LLM in the task without consuming context budget: a subtitles list of {start, end, text} dictionaries (or None when unavailable), and video metadata exposed via get_video_info() (duration, resolu-tion, frame rate, shot count). These initial variables let the LLM scope the problem before issuing any query and act as a free, always-available textual information about the video. Crucially, the REPL is persistent across turns: the LLM can accumu-late evidence in arbitrary Python variables (e.g., 

evidence_by_option = {} ), revisit and refine retrieved segments, and compose primitives into multi-step strategies that span many iterations. This persistence is what makes adaptive, code-driven probing tractable. Specifically, intermediate find-ings do not need to be re-derived, and the LLM’s reasoning state is externalized into inspectable vari-ables rather than carried latently inside a single forward pass. B.1 Extended Results on Benchmark Datasets 

Table 4 reports results on the MLVU benchmark, which evaluates long-video understanding across three categories: holistic reasoning (Topic Reason-ing or TR, Anomaly Recognition or AR), single-detail queries (Neddle QA or NQA, Ego Reason-ing or ER, Plot Question or PQA), and multi-detail reasoning (Action Order or AO, Action Count or AC). Our approach achieves a 69.3 overall aver-age, substantially outperforming the baseline VLM (54.5) and surpassing strong open-source and pro-prietary models such as LLaVA-OneVision (51.7) and GPT-4o (54.5). The improvements are particu-larly pronounced in multi-detail reasoning, where our method achieves 81.1 on Action Order and 53.9 on Action Count, far exceeding prior meth-ods that typically remain below 50. These tasks require aggregating multiple pieces of information across distant segments, which standard end-to-end VLMs struggle with due to temporal redun-dancy and limited reasoning structure. By contrast, HPP’s hierarchical probing allows the model to it-eratively localize relevant segments and combine evidence across time. We also observe strong gains on single-detail tasks, including Needle QA (70.4) and Plot QA (70.9), demonstrating improved tem-poral grounding and question-specific retrieval. 

B.2 Visualization of Reasoning Traces 

Our framework answers queries by iteratively in-teracting with the video through a programmatic interface. During inference, the LLM plans actions, writes and executes code in the REPL, invokes probing functions, and combines the returned infor-mation with its internal reasoning to progressively refine the answer. To make this process transpar-ent and easier to inspect, we provide an interactive visualization application as part of supplementary code. The information on how to run the application attached in Readme file in the code. Running the application launches a dashboard that summarizes key statistics about the model’s execution traces across datasets. Users can select a dataset and split to explore individual reasoning sessions in detail. Each session can be viewed from two complemen-tary perspectives: a functional view that highlights the sequence of probing function calls and retrieved information, and a coding view that displays the generated code and its execution results. These vi-sualizations allow readers to follow how the model explores the video, narrows down candidate seg-ments, and gathers evidence before producing the final answer.

D Extended Related Work 

D.1 Exploiting Temporal Redundancy and Structured Video Representations 

Long videos contain substantial temporal redun-dancy, making direct frame-level processing inef-ficient. Video segmentation methods aim to par-tition videos into semantically or temporally co-herent units such as shots or scenes, which serve as higher-level structural representations for down-stream tasks (Sun et al., 2025). These segment-level abstractions enable more efficient indexing, retrieval, and reasoning compared to raw frame sequences. A closely related area is video compression, which explicitly exploits temporal redundancy to reduce storage and transmission costs. Early com-pression techniques focused primarily on frame-level encoding, whereas modern video codecs incorporate motion estimation and motion com-pensation to model temporal dependencies across frames (Musmann et al., 2005). In typical com-pression pipelines, a subset of frames known as intra-coded frames (I-frames) are stored indepen-dently, while intermediate frames are reconstructed through motion vectors and residual information relative to reference frames. From an information-theoretic perspective, this process reduces redun-dancy by encoding only the incremental informa-tion necessary to reconstruct successive frames. Although designed for compression, these repre-sentations implicitly capture important structural cues. For instance, motion vectors have been used as efficient approximations of optical flow, while I-frames often correlate with visually salient keyframes (Ai et al., 2023). Prior work has lever-aged such compression signals to accelerate video processing pipelines or approximate motion dynam-ics (Wu et al., 2018; Jin et al., 2024). In contrast to prior work that primarily uses compression arti-facts for computational acceleration, we leverage temporal redundancy as a structural prior for hier-archical video decomposition. D.2 Moment Localization and Retrievalin Videos 

Video moment localization aims to identify tempo-ral segments within long videos that correspond to a natural language query. Early approaches train task-specific architectures that align frame-level visual representations with textual embed-dings through cross-modal objectives (Anne Hen-dricks et al., 2017; Gao et al., 2017a; Wu and Han, 2018; Liu et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021). These methods typically rely on densely sampled frames and specialized supervision sig-nals designed for benchmark datasets. More recent research has explored generalized retrieval frame-works that reduce reliance on task-specific train-ing and improve cross-domain transferability (Lin et al., 2023b; Lei et al., 2021; Ren et al., 2023). These approaches aim to unify retrieval and local-ization tasks by learning more flexible video–text representations. A closely related problem is text-based video retrieval, where the goal is to retrieve relevant videos given a natural language query. Early works extend contrastive image–text pretraining meth-ods such as CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) to the video domain by aggregating frame-level repre-sentations (Zhang et al., 2024a; Xu et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023b). Subsequent models train large-scale video–text alignment systems specifically de-signed for video retrieval, achieving substantially stronger performance (Wang et al., 2024a; Halbe et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2024c). Existing methods for language-based video localization often rely on dense captioning pipelines or classifier-based feature extraction (Gao et al., 2017b; Huang et al., 2024). While effective for narrow tasks, these ap-21 proaches typically require exhaustive frame-level encoding and predefined alignment procedures. In contrast, our framework employs pretrained video–text retrieval models as semantic search modules within a hierarchical video representa-tion. Rather than performing exhaustive frame-level matching, retrieval operates over structured temporal segments derived from our hierarchy, enabling scalable and query-adaptive localization within long videos. 

D.3 Long Video Understanding with Multimodal LLMs 

Understanding long-form videos remains a major challenge for multimodal large language models (MLLMs) (Li et al., 2024a, 2023a,c; Shen et al., 2024). Unlike short clips, long videos contain com-plex temporal dependencies, variable information density, and massive input sizes that strain current architectures. One common strategy involves token compres-sion techniques that reduce redundancy in visual representations (Li et al.; Hu et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025a; Li et al., 2024c; Jiang et al., 2025). These methods merge similar visual tokens or ap-ply aggressive compression ratios to fit long videos within limited context windows (Shen et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2023). However, such compression often discards fine-grained visual details, leading to degraded performance on tasks requiring precise temporal reasoning. Another direction focuses on extending the con-text windows of large language models to support longer multimodal inputs (Zhang et al., 2024c). While larger context windows enable models to process longer sequences, they do not inherently solve the challenge of efficiently locating relevant information within long videos. Retrieval-based approaches attempt to address this limitation by se-lectively identifying informative segments. For ex-ample, VideoTree (Wang et al., 2025c) constructs a hierarchical tree over frame-level representations to guide search through long videos. However, build-ing such structures requires expensive frame-level clustering and captioning. Similarly, VCA (Yang et al., 2025c) formulates video exploration as a reinforcement learning problem where an agent navigates a graph representation of the video to identify relevant segments. Another line of work focuses on adaptive sam-pling and iterative refinement. D.4 Agent-based Long Video Understanding. 

Recent approaches treat video understanding as an agentic process in which models interact with exter-nal tools to retrieve and analyze relevant informa-tion (Wang et al., 2025c; Yang et al., 2025c; Wang et al., 2024b). Early frameworks rely on retrieval tools such as CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) to identify key frames (Li et al.), though they often struggle with domain gaps and limited temporal coverage. LVAgent (Chen et al., 2025) introduces multi-agent collaboration among multimodal models, enabling 22 dynamic team selection and iterative retrieval. Our approach differs from these systems in two key as-pects. First, we employ a single coding-capable LLM that performs strategy planning and iterative exploration within a programmatic execution REPL environment. Second, rather than relying on dense captioning pipelines or multiple specialized agents, our framework uses lightweight perceptual mod-ules combined with hierarchical retrieval, enabling efficient and flexible reasoning over long videos. 

E Emergent Coding and Probing Functions-based Strategy Patterns 

An important aspect of our method, HPP, is its ability to flexibly combine probing functions with a code execution environment (REPL) to analyze videos for efficient information extraction. This integration enables our method to iteratively query visual information and programmatically process intermediate results, leading to more structured and interpretable reasoning over complex video data. In practice, we observe that HPP leverages these components in several unique and creative ways, forming distinct reasoning patterns during infer-ence. To better understand these behaviors, we ana-lyze the reasoning traces generated by our method and document representative samples here. To facilitate further inspection and reproducibil-ity, we provide an interactive visualization tool in the attached code of supplementary material that allows readers to explore the reasoning traces in detail. 

E.1 Emergent Pattern 1: Hierarchical Coarse-to-Fine Localization 

As shown in Listing 2, our method is able to uti-lize hierarchical structure functions for coarse-to-fine localization. Rather than searching the full video at a uniform granularity, our method indepen-dently discovers a three-stage pyramid: (1) seman-tic search at the scene level to identify candidate temporal regions; (2) refinement at the shot level within those regions; (3) dense frame sampling within the top-scoring shots for VLM verification. This mirrors coarse-to-fine search strategies in clas-sical computer vision (Burt and Adelson, 1987) but is constructed entirely from probing function com-positions in the code. The VLM calls only on the most promising temporal regions. This emergent efficiency is never instructed.

E.4 Pattern 4: Temporal Non-Maximum Suppression for Event Counting 

In counting tasks, our method (HPP) implements an explicit temporal clustering algorithm: (1) collect candidate event timestamps via multi-query search; (2) sort chronologically; (3) apply a gap-threshold merge (typically 1-2 s) to collapse adjacent shots that belong to the same event occurrence into a single cluster; (4) verify each cluster with VLM; (5) count verified clusters as the answer. This con-stitutes a spontaneous re-implementation of tempo-ral non-maximum suppression. The 1-second gap threshold is chosen heuristically and varies across questions (0.5–2s depending on action type), sug-gesting it reasons about action granularity. This is a spontaneous re-implementation of temporal non-maximum suppression (Brown et al., 2014).

System Prompt. In this section, we briefly dis-cuss the system prompt used in our framework. As discussed in the main paper, the prompt is built upon the Recursive Language Model (RLM) frame-work (Zhang et al., 2025a) and is adapted for long-form video understanding. Specifically, we extend the original formulation with video-specific instruc-tions and structured guidance that help the model plan exploration strategies and efficiently interact with the video through the REPL. The prompt begins with a high-level description of the task and the overall reasoning setting. It then introduces the REPL environment, including the accessible variables and the available probing func-tions that the model can invoke to inspect different parts of the video. In addition, the prompt provides several strategy hints and operational guidelines that encourage the model to reason about the video structure, progressively refine its search, and avoid unnecessary visual queries. Finally, the prompt includes a set of explicit rules together with a short example illustrating how the probing functions can be combined during reasoning. These components guide the LLM to formulate structured exploration strategies and effectively navigate long videos dur-ing inference. 25
